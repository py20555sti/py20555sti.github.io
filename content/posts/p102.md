Somehow, spring completely caught me off guard this year, and the Rallye Boží
Bojovníci didn't take place at its traditional early-spring date.

Better late than never...

And so, at the end of September, armed with stickers - an indispensable part of
our rally - I set off by train to Tomek's place, where the Impreza has now taken
up residence.

This edition was a return to the roots of the Boží Bojovníci, to the borderlands
of Czechia, Poland and Germany, stretching from Teplice nad Metují to Dresden.

We arrive in Adršpach as the first visitors of the day, and not without a good
reason: the narrow, winding roads are exactly the sort of thing the Impreza
likes best. The early start pays off, and we practically have the place to
ourselves. The weather decides to play along too, shifting from ominous mists
concealing the spirit of Gelfrad von Stercz to glorious autumn sunshine.

Every time I visit the Czech Republic, I find myself thinking that there is
something deeply appealing about that country. At first glance, Poland and the
Czech Republic seem very much alike. We are sharing a border, a region, and much
of our history. And yet I can't shake the feeling that our respective fates were
shaped by two entirely different currents of history.

In Poland, you can still sense the Soviet imprint - the trace of Russian
domination, almost ingrained beneath the surface, the organisation of public
space, that deep-rooted mistrust of the East. In Czechia, I don't feel the same
thing. Instead, I sense the legacy of the empire: Habsburg order. It's as though
that Central European character, that bourgeois culture, that continuity of the
society developed over centuries within a multinational monarchy, were still
there, intact.

You may have your feet up on a bench, soaking up the sun, but you can still feel
the empire:

Our adventure came to an end in Dresden, a city that, like few others in this
part of Europe, bears the scars of history. And yet, with a stubbornness and no
small degree of elegance, strives to preserve its former glory. This was quite a
surprise to me, a troglodyte freshly emerged from beneath a rock, in the
Subcarpathian backwoods.
